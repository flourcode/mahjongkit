Mahjong Tile Materials: Melamine vs. Acrylic vs. Porcelain

Melamine is the durable standard for play, acrylic is the designer option with decorative backs, and porcelain sets are mostly for display. Here is how they compare in the hand and on the table.

Updated September 20266 min readMahjongKit Editorial Team

  2. Acrylic: the designer choice
  3. Porcelain and ceramic: mostly for display
  4. A note on antique bone-and-bamboo sets
  5. Comparison

The material of your tiles decides how the set sounds when you wash it, how it feels in the hand and how it ages. Because every hand starts with 152 tiles being scrubbed against each other face down, the material has to handle friction well. Three materials cover almost everything on sale today.

Melamine: the standard for play

Most club and tournament sets are melamine, a dense thermosetting plastic.

  • Feel: heavy and solid. Tiles sit firmly on the rack and rarely tip.
  • Sound: the sharp, satisfying clack most people associate with Mahjong.
  • Durability: generally very durable and resistant to ordinary game-night wear. Engraved, painted faces hold up well for years of weekly play.
  • Looks: opaque, usually white, ivory or a warm antique tone.

Acrylic: the designer choice

As Mahjong has grown with younger players, acrylic has taken over the decorative end of the market because it can be cast in layers and colors.

  • Feel: usually a bit lighter than melamine, with crisper edges. Premium blocks can still feel substantial.
  • Sound: pleasant but slightly higher-pitched than melamine.
  • Durability: softer than melamine, so faces and backs can pick up fine scratches over time. Not fragile, just not as hard-wearing.
  • Looks: the reason to buy it. Patterned, translucent or layered backs are possible in ways melamine cannot match.

Porcelain and ceramic: mostly for display

Beginners sometimes assume a "traditional" set must be porcelain. Historically, sets were bone and bamboo; ceramic sets are a modern novelty.

  • Feel: heavy and cool, like fine china.
  • Sound: a glassy clink that many players find harsh during a long wash.
  • Durability: poor for regular play. Ceramic can chip or crack when tiles collide during the wash or fall off the table.
  • Verdict: if a set is advertised as porcelain, it is either melamine styled to look like porcelain, or a display piece. Ask before you buy it to play with.

A note on antique bone-and-bamboo sets

Vintage sets with bone faces dovetailed onto bamboo backs are beautiful and collectible, but many predate Jokers and use different tile counts. Unless it has been supplemented with Jokers and matches 152 tiles, an antique set is a collector's item rather than an American playing set.

Which should you buy?

For a first set that will be played often, melamine. For a set that is partly a decoration or a gift, acrylic. Skip porcelain unless it is going on a shelf. Then read our guide to American sets for the other things to check, such as Jokers, pushers and readability.
